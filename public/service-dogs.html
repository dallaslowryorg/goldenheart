Service Dog Programs

Individual training for individual lives.

Golden Heart's service-dog program is built around the person, the dog, and the work they will do together. Program level is based on task complexity, training needs, environment, handler preparation, and the dog's individual temperament and abilities—not diagnosis alone.

Three service-dog levels

Training scope grows with the complexity of the work.

Every client is evaluated individually before a final program and price are determined.

Level One

Psychiatric, PTSD & Autism Support

$7,500–$10,500

Level One is designed for service-dog needs that require reliable obedience, strong public-access skills, and a defined set of disability-related tasks, but generally involve a less complex training program.

Typical scope: basic-to-moderate task complexity + strong public access + reliable obedience + individualized handler training.

Examples of Level One work

  • Basic grounding through trained contact
  • Tactile stimulation
  • Positioning alongside the handler for trained support
  • Creating space around the handler through positioning
  • Basic anxiety or psychiatric interruption tasks
  • Deep pressure therapy
  • Block command
  • Barrier command for public space

Level Two

Moderate Mobility & More Complex Support

$12,500–$16,000

Level Two involves more extensive task training, public-access work, environmental exposure, and individualized preparation. It may be appropriate for moderate mobility needs, more complex autism support, POTS-related work, and other cases requiring a broader task plan.

Level Two may include applicable Level One work plus more advanced or combined task sequences.

Examples of Level Two work

  • Multiple psychiatric interruption tasks
  • Advanced grounding or disruption sequences
  • Interrupting self-injurious or repetitive behaviors
  • Interrupting dissociation
  • Waking a handler from nightmares
  • Guiding a handler to a safe location
  • Finding a specific person
  • Very limited, appropriately evaluated bracing or stability support
  • Assistance getting up from certain positions when appropriate
  • Creating a physical buffer in crowded environments
  • Complex autism-related interruption and redirection
  • Mobility assistance with steps or stairs when appropriate
  • Tethering or grounding for elopement-related autism support

Level Three

Medical Alert, Advanced Mobility & Complex Task Work

$18,000–$22,000

Level Three is Golden Heart's highest level of individualized service-dog training and is intended for cases requiring significant specialized task work, advanced public access, extensive environmental preparation, and a higher level of individualized support.

Typical scope: complex or specialized task work + extensive public access + significant individualized training + advanced team preparation.

Examples of Level Three work

  • Multiple-step task chains and complex psychiatric response sequences
  • Advanced dissociation, panic, and anxiety response
  • Finding or retrieving emergency medication or supplies
  • Medical alert or response behaviors when the dog demonstrates reliable ability
  • Allergy alert or diabetes alert when appropriate and reliably trained
  • Advanced autism support
  • Safety-oriented environmental responses
  • Advanced mobility-related assistance when medically and physically appropriate
  • Retrieval of objects during a disability episode
  • Assistance with controlled transitions between positions
  • Opening or closing doors and specialized item retrieval
  • Advanced environmental navigation and wheelchair navigation commands
  • Complex combinations of individualized disability-related tasks

How level is determined

The diagnosis alone does not decide the program.

Golden Heart considers the person's actual disability-related needs, the tasks required, the dog's temperament and abilities, the amount and complexity of training, the environment where the dog will work, and the level of handler education and transition support needed.

Each client is evaluated individually before a final program and price are determined.

Program dogs

Golden Heart provides the dog.

Golden Heart does not train a client's existing dog through the service-dog program. Dogs are selected and provided through Golden Heart so temperament, health, training progress, and suitability can be evaluated throughout the program.

What may be included

A complete working-team program.

Specific inclusions are defined in each client's individual contract.

Dog Development

  • Professional obedience training
  • House manners and socialization
  • Public-access preparation
  • Environmental exposure and distraction training
  • Veterinary care appropriate to the program

Task & Team Training

  • Disability-specific task training
  • Customized training based on client needs
  • Handler education
  • Team training and real-world practice
  • Transition training

After Placement

  • Transition assistance after the dog goes home
  • Follow-up communication
  • Training questions and troubleshooting
  • Refresher guidance
  • Ongoing support for the working partnership

Payment options

Payment schedules are individualized.

Golden Heart does not require a client to begin making payments before the initial documentation and intake process has been reviewed and the client has been accepted into the program.

Depending on the dog, placement, organization, travel requirements, and contract, payment structures may include:

  • 50% at contract signing and 50% at completion
  • 100% at contract signing
  • 65% during the contract-signing month, then monthly installments through completion
  • 50% at contract signing, then monthly installments through completion

Program property & refunds

The individual contract controls.

Once a dog is approximately halfway through its contracted program, payments are generally non-refundable because substantial training, veterinary care, food, equipment, and other program expenses have already been invested.

The dog remains program property until graduation or transfer according to the individual contract.

Exact payment, refund, ownership, and transfer terms are always governed by the signed service agreement.

Training timeframe

The dog graduates when the team is ready.

Training time varies based on the dog's age, starting level, temperament, tasks, and the complexity of the client's needs. Programs generally involve several months to more than a year of professional training.

Some dogs may require approximately 8–10 months or longer, and specialized placements can take a year or more.

No rushed timelines

Readiness matters more than a calendar date.

The goal is not to move a dog through a predetermined number of months. Graduation happens when the dog and handler are appropriately prepared for the working partnership.

Long-distance clients

Distance does not automatically rule out a placement.

Golden Heart is based in Tulsa, Oklahoma. Clients outside the local area are considered individually, and travel arrangements are discussed before a contract is finalized.

  • Within 500 miles of Tulsa: $3,500 travel fee
  • Within 900 miles of Tulsa: $4,200 travel fee
  • Beyond approximately 900 miles: custom travel or delivery arrangement
  • International travel: typically about $5,500–$8,000, depending on distance and logistics

Long-distance plans may include delivery of the service dog, travel to the client's location, in-person team training, remote follow-up, and additional transition support.

Mileage is calculated from Tulsa, Oklahoma. Final travel, transportation, lodging, airfare, and transition costs are discussed before the agreement is finalized.

Additional information for long-distance clients

Golden Heart may request additional information before accepting a long-distance placement, including photos or videos of the home and environment, the client's daily routine, veterinary information, and other financial or program documentation.

Why the transition plan matters

The right plan depends on the dog, handler, disability-related needs, home environment, distance, and the amount of in-person training needed for a successful transition.

Forever Care

Placement is not the finish line.

Golden Heart's goal is to remain available after placement rather than simply sending a dog home and ending the relationship.

Continued Guidance

Follow-up communication, training questions, handler education, and troubleshooting can remain part of the relationship.

Transition & Refresher Support

Golden Heart may provide transition support, refresher guidance, and assistance when the dog's work or the team's needs change.

Additional Training

More extensive in-person refresher training, additional task training, or substantial retraining may be charged separately depending on what is needed.

Children & minors

Pediatric placements are evaluated individually.

Golden Heart accepts pediatric placements, including autism-support placements, when a service dog is an appropriate fit for the child and family. A parent or legal guardian must participate in the application, decisions, training, and ongoing care of the dog.

Children under age 13, and clients whose disability makes additional assistance necessary, will have a designated secondary handler involved in the working team.

Medical alert & response

Individual ability comes first.

Medical alert and response tasks are individually evaluated and based on the dog's demonstrated abilities, temperament, training, and the specific needs of the handler. Examples may be discussed during evaluation, but no individual dog is represented as guaranteed to perform every type of medical alert.

Retirement planning

Forever Care continues through retirement.

Service-dog retirement varies by the individual dog and commonly occurs around 8–11 years of age depending on health, ability, and the demands of the work. When retirement approaches, Golden Heart can help the handler plan for the transition and, when appropriate, a successor dog.

Staying with the family

In most cases, Golden Heart's preference is for a retired service dog to remain with the handler when that is in the dog's best interest and the handler can continue providing appropriate care. Retirement decisions are made around the welfare of the dog and the needs of the team.
